# Failure patterns

Each pattern: what it looks like, why it fails, before and after, and the rule it breaks.
All examples are invented for teaching; no real product, company, study or person.
"Lumivra", "Zelorastat", "ZELORA-2", "ZELORA-3", "LUMI-1" and "Brand A" are fictional.

## Contents

- Fair balance FB1–FB8 (task TR-3.1.1)
- Selective reporting SR1–SR8 (task TR-3.1.2)

Pattern ids are for this file only. In what the person reads, describe the problem in
plain words (TR-H13).

## Fair balance

**FB1 The benefits-only slide.** Efficacy, convenience and outcomes, and nothing on side
effects, discontinuations or limitations.
Why: the reader forms a view of the product from half the evidence; reviewers send it
back first.
Before: "Lumivra: 72% clear or almost clear skin at week 16. Once-monthly dosing."
After: "Lumivra: 72% clear or almost clear skin at week 16, versus 9% on placebo (LUMI-1).
Most common side effects: upper respiratory infection (12% vs 8%) and injection-site
reactions (7% vs 2%); 3% stopped treatment because of side effects (vs 1%)." (R1, R2, R4,
R7)

**FB2 Safety in the footnote.** The benefit in the headline, the risks in small type,
an appendix, the back page or "see prescribing information".
Why: the reader of the headline does not read the footnote; claims should stand alone.
After: the main risks in the body, beside the benefit; the footnote or full label adds
detail. (R2, R4)

**FB3 The reassuring word.** "Safe", "well tolerated", "favorable safety profile", "no
side effects", "reassuring safety" in the draft's own voice.
Why: these are conclusions, not data; promotional codes restrict them; the reader cannot
weigh them.
Before: "Zelorastat was well tolerated."
After: "Serious adverse events occurred in 11% of patients on zelorastat and 10% on
placebo; 9% and 4% stopped because of side effects." Or, if the source uses the phrase:
"The authors described zelorastat as well tolerated; serious adverse events occurred in
11% and 10%." (R5, R7)

**FB4 "Similar to placebo".** A safety comparison stated as equivalence when the figures
differ, or when the study was not designed to compare safety.
Before: "Side effects were similar to placebo."
After: give both groups' rates for the main events and for discontinuation due to side
effects; let the reader see the difference. (R1, R8)

**FB5 The dropped discontinuations.** Efficacy and value shown; the share of patients who
stopped treatment, or stopped because of side effects, left out.
Why: for a payer, dropout changes the real-world value; for a doctor, it is part of the
safety picture.
Before: "Brand A delivers 8 fewer admissions per 100 patients per year."
After: "… in the registry; 22% of patients stopped Brand A within 12 months (14% on
standard care), which the value figure does not account for [GAP: needs a decision —
whether the model adjusts for discontinuation — ask the health economics lead]." (R8,
R10)

**FB6 The missing limitation.** Open-label design, short follow-up, a single study, a
restricted population, immature data: stated by the authors, dropped by the draft.
After: one plain line of the limitation the source states, near the result it qualifies.
(R4, R8, R10)

**FB7 The grouped side effect.** "Some stomach upset" in place of the specific events and
rates the source lists.
After: name the events as the source does, with their rates. (R3)

**FB8 Balance in another piece.** "The safety is in the medical deck", "we'll cover side
effects in the follow-up email".
Why: each piece can be read, forwarded or printed on its own.
After: each piece carries its own main risks; the other piece adds detail. (R1, R4)

## Selective reporting

**SR1 The missing trial.** Two trials of the same product and use in the material; the
draft reports only the one that met its primary endpoint.
Before: "ZELORA-2 showed zelorastat reduced major events (HR 0.66)."
After: "In ZELORA-2, zelorastat reduced major events (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.97). In
ZELORA-3, the primary endpoint was not met (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.70 to 1.18)." (R3, R4, R7,
R10)

**SR2 The buried primary.** A secondary or exploratory result leads; the missed primary
endpoint appears last, in a footnote, or not at all.
After: lead with the primary result as reported; give secondary results as secondary.
(R9, R4)

**SR3 The best time point.** The week where the effect peaked, when the pre-specified
time point shows a smaller or no difference.
Before: "Response 48% vs 20% at week 8." (Primary at week 24: 35% vs 28%.)
After: "At week 24, the pre-specified time point, response was 35% vs 28%; at week 8 it
was 48% vs 20%." (R9, R3)

**SR4 The subgroup headline.** A favorable subgroup, often post hoc, given as the main
message while the overall result is weaker or absent.
After: overall result first; subgroup labeled, with whether it was pre-specified. (R3,
R9)

**SR5 Completers and responders only.** Results among patients who finished or responded,
presented as if for everyone randomized.
After: the result in everyone it was planned for, then the completer or responder
analysis labeled as such, with how many dropped out. (R8, R9)

**SR6 The favorable authority alone.** A guideline's or expert's positive line quoted;
the same source's caveat or a later, less favorable statement left out.
After: quote the caveat too, or do not quote. (R3)

**SR7 The "up to" figure.** The best result across arms, doses, sites or studies given
as the result.
Before: "Up to 80% response." After: the result of the approved dose in the primary
analysis, as reported. (R3, R4)

**SR8 The unstated scope.** A legitimate focus (one endpoint, one study) presented with no
word that more exists.
After: "This slide covers the primary endpoint of ZELORA-2; secondary endpoints and
safety follow." (R4, R10)
