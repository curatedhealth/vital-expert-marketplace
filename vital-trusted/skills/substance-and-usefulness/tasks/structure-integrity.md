## What counts as a structure problem

- **The invented framework:** a 2x2, a matrix, a pyramid, "three pillars" or a named
  model ("the ACCESS framework") that the brief did not ask for and the content does not
  need. The tell: axes that are not defined or measured, quadrants filled by judgment
  the material does not record, or a label that makes a list look like an analysis.
- **Buckets forced on the content:** items squeezed into three or four groups so the
  page looks balanced; a group with one item in it, or one item that fits two groups.
- **Headings that carry no meaning:** "Overview", "Key considerations", "Strategic
  landscape", "Next steps" over a section that holds no steps.
- **A heading that does not match its section:** "Risks" over a section that lists
  opportunities; "Recommendation" over a summary of facts.
- **Points out of order for the content:** the conclusion after the evidence that
  depends on it is fine; the conclusion buried in the last bullet of the third slide is
  not.

## When a framework is right

When the person asks for one, or the content genuinely has two measured dimensions (for
example, supplied scores on two defined criteria), a framework helps. Build it only from
the supplied content: axes defined in words from the material, each item placed by data
or by the person's own stated judgment, and the source of each placement visible. Never
place an item by the assistant's own judgment and present it as analysis.

## Guard (while drafting)

1. **Start from the content, not a template.** List the points the material supports,
   then group them only if the grouping helps the reader act.
2. **Use the structure the brief asked for.** If the brief asked for "three priorities",
   write three priorities, not a matrix.
3. **Write headings as the point of the section** where the reader skims: "Payer review
   in canton X is the main risk to the Q1 target", not "Market access update".
4. **Do not add a framework to look strategic.** If a framework would help, offer it in
   one line and let the person decide.

## Repair (existing draft)

1. **Compare the structure with the brief and the content.** For each framework, ask:
   did the brief ask for it, are its dimensions defined, does the material support each
   placement?
2. **Remove or flag an invented framework.** Replace it with the structure the content
   supports (often a ranked list). Where the person may want to keep it, flag it and say
   what it would need: defined axes and a source for each placement.
3. **Fix headings** that say nothing or do not match their section. Rewrite them from
   the section's own content; do not add a claim in the heading that the section does
   not support.
4. **Reorder** only where the order hides the main point or splits a point from its
   support. Note each move.
5. **List structural changes** in the repair note, including any framework removed.

## Check line

Feeds the structure line in `assets/check-line.md`: headings that do not match their
content, frameworks or groupings the content does not support, points out of order.

## Boundaries

- Whether the format suits the reader (memo or slides, length, an executive summary) is
  `fit-for-reader`. This task asks whether the structure follows the content.
- Slide design, typography, numbering style and the company template are style and out
  of scope (TR-H9).
- A framework the company uses as standard (for example a mandated business review
  template) is the person's choice; do not remove it. Check only that each section
  holds what its heading says.
