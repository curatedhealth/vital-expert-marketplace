# Primary rules behind this skill

Each rule names its primary source, its date, its scope and how it was confirmed. A rule
marked `[CHECK]` has not been read from the primary document, or its currency or scope
is open; it is applied as good practice, never cited as a requirement (TR-H8).

Laws bind in their own jurisdiction. Industry codes bind their members, and companies
that agree to them, in the markets they cover. Outside that scope a rule here is good
practice only. Which rules apply to a given piece of work is decided by the company's
own promotional or medical review process, not by this skill (TR-H6, TR-H11).

Quotations are short extracts read through a fetch tool, which limits each quote to a
short span. Where a provision is longer, the wording is summarized and marked as such.

## 6. How to use these rules in output

- In what the person reads, say the practice in plain words: "your label covers adults
  only; this message reaches beyond it", "a 'better than' claim needs a study that
  compared the two directly". Name a rule only if the person asks (TR-H13).
- Keep the scope: R1 to R3, R10, R11 are EU law; R4, R16, R17, R27 are US; R5, R12,
  R18 to R20 are the UK code; R6, R13, R21 the EFPIA code; R7, R14, R22 the IFPMA
  baseline; R8, R9, R15, R23, R24 Swiss law; R25, R26 EU devices.
- Where the market is not stated, do not pick one. The same pattern holds across the
  confirmed sources (promote only within the approved label; compare like with like
  on substantiated data; do not disparage), so the method runs as good practice and the
  note asks for the market (TR-H7).
- Never say a draft meets a rule, is on-label, is non-promotional or is a fair
  comparison. Say what goes beyond the supplied label, what reads as promotion, which
  comparisons have no direct source, and who decides (TR-H3, TR-H11).

## Label and intended use

**L1 The widened population.** A message reaches patients the label does not cover.
Why: the label states who the product is for; a message to a wider group suggests a use
that was not approved.
Label: adults with moderate to severe condition X. Before: "Brevanzo: control for
patients with condition X." After: "Brevanzo: for adults with moderate to severe
condition X." (R2, R4, R5, R6, R8)

**L2 The field insight that became a message.** What doctors are doing in practice is
turned into a key message.
Why: reported practice is not an approved use; a message built on it promotes that use.
Before (request): "Doctors are using it in teenagers, add a message for that." After: no
message; one plain line to the person that teenagers are outside the label they
supplied, and that questions doctors raise go to medical information. (R4, R5, R6, R7)

**L3 The implied use.** No unapproved use is named, but the wording points to one.
Why: "suggest" is enough under the US rule, and the UK and EU codes judge the
impression, not only the words.
Before: "Brevanzo: ready when your patients need more than first-line options" (label:
after at least one prior therapy). After: "Brevanzo: for adults with condition X who
have had at least one prior therapy." Wording of the line of therapy kept to the label.
(R4, R5)

**L4 The rounded label.** Label wording is paraphrased into something broader.
Why: "symptomatic relief of condition X" and "treats condition X" are different claims.
After: keep the label's own wording for indication, population, dose and setting; plain
language is fine elsewhere. (R2, R5, R8)

**L5 The other market's label.** A use approved in one market appears in material for
another.
Why: approvals differ by market and date; the label for this market is the boundary.
After: ask which market; use that market's label; mark the difference as
`[GAP: conflicting — …]` if both labels were supplied. (R1, R7, R8)

**L6 The pipeline preview.** An investigational product or indication appears in
promotional material "as coming soon".
Why: promotion before authorization is prohibited in every confirmed source.
After: remove from promotional material; scientific exchange about it goes through the
medical route. (R1, R5, R6, R7)

**L7 The device that does more.** App or device copy describes functions beyond its
intended purpose: "diagnoses", "detects", "predicts", "rules out", "replaces a visit".
Why: claims beyond the intended purpose are themselves prohibited for devices, and in
the US claims can show a new intended use.
Before: "Ventrelo Rhythm diagnoses atrial fibrillation." Intended purpose: "records a
single-lead ECG and notifies the user of an irregular rhythm that may need review by a
doctor." After: "Ventrelo Rhythm records your heart rhythm and lets you know when it
finds an irregular rhythm, so you can share it with your doctor." (R25, R26, R27)

**L8 The label from memory.** The assistant states the indication or dose without the
label having been supplied.
Why: the reviewer will check against the current label; a remembered one may be out of
date or from another market.
After: ask for the label; until then `[GAP: not supplied — approved indication for
<market> — supply the current label]`. (TR-H1, TR-H7)

## Promotional boundary

**P1 The reply that sells.** A reply to a doctor's question carries brand slogans,
unrequested benefits or a call to prescribe.
Why: the exemption for replies holds only when the reply relates solely to the
question, is accurate, not misleading and not promotional.
Before: "Great question! Brevanzo is the trusted choice for … Here is our latest data."
After: restate the question, answer only it, with the data and its limits, no slogans.
(R10, R12, R13, R14)

**P2 The prompted question.** A rep asks a doctor to "send a question to medical" so
off-label data can be sent.
Why: an enquiry prompted by the company is not unsolicited.
After: the skill does not draft the prompt or the reply; it names the medical
information procedure and the person's review process. (R12)

**P3 The reply that grows.** The answer covers other uses, other products or
competitor weaknesses the doctor did not ask about.
Why: a reply must relate solely to the question.
After: answer the question asked; offer further information only in the way the
company's procedure allows. (R12)

**P4 The disease page with a hint.** Disease information for the public names, shows or
clearly points to the company's product.
Why: disease information is outside advertising rules only when there is no reference,
even indirect, to medicinal products; prescription medicines may not be advertised to
the public in the EU.
Before: "Ask your doctor about new once-weekly options for condition X" (the company's
product is the only once-weekly one). After: remove the indirect pointer; route the
page to promotional review. (R10, R11, R15)

**P5 The tone shift.** Information is written as an advertisement: "game-changing",
"proven", "breakthrough", exclamation marks, a call to action.
Why: tone is part of what makes a piece promotional; scientific exchange that reads as
an advertisement will be treated as one.
After: neutral verbs ("in the trial, …"), results with their limits, no call to action.
(R3, R12, R20)

**P6 The wrong audience.** Professional material reused for patients or the public.
Why: different rules apply to each audience, and prescription medicines may not be
advertised to the public in the EU and Switzerland.
After: ask who will read it; patient material goes through its own review. (R11, R15)

**P7 The hidden promotion.** Promotional material presented as an article, a study
summary or an independent view.
Why: advertising must be recognizable as advertising (Swiss rule confirmed; elsewhere
good practice). After: label the piece for what it is; review decides the format.
(R9; outside Switzerland, good practice)

**P8 The scientific-exchange label as a shield.** A piece is called "scientific
exchange" or "medical education" to avoid promotional review.
Why: what a piece is depends on content, intent and use, not its label. This skill
does not decide which it is.
After: record the use as the person states it; route to the review process that
decides. (TR-H6, TR-H11)

## Comparative claims

**C1 Better with no head-to-head.** "Better", "more effective", "faster" or "safer" than
a named competitor without a study that compared the two directly.
Why: a comparison needs substantial evidence (US) or substantiable, representative,
like-for-like data (UK, EFPIA, Switzerland); placebo-controlled trials of each product
do not compare them with each other.
Before: "Brevanzo works faster than Kelandro." After: "[GAP: not supplied — a study
comparing Brevanzo with Kelandro on time to response — supply it; without it, remove
the comparison]". (R17, R18, R21, R23)

**C2 The cross-trial comparison.** Results from two separate trials set side by side as
if from one.
Why: different populations, endpoints and time points; not like with like.
After: keep each result with its own trial and comparator, no "versus" between them,
and a gap or a question for medical review on whether any comparison can be shown at
all. (R18, R19, R23)

**C3 The unqualified "first" or "only".** "The first", "the only", "the leading" with no
stated scope, date or measure.
Why: absolute claims need adequate qualification and substantiation; superlatives only
where they state a clear fact.
Before: "The only once-weekly treatment." After: "Once-weekly dosing" or a scoped,
dated, sourced statement the person supplies ("the only once-weekly treatment approved
for condition X in <market> as of <date>, per <source>"). (R20, R21, R22)

**C4 The comparison on the wrong measure.** A comparison chooses a feature that is not
material or representative (dosing convenience presented as superior outcomes).
Why: comparisons must be of material, relevant, substantiable and representative
features. After: say exactly which feature is compared and on what data. (R18, R21)

**C5 The disparaging line.** The competitor is mocked, belittled or blamed: "outdated",
"risky", "old-fashioned", "patients deserve better than Kelandro".
Why: other companies' products must not be disparaged; comparative advertising must not
be disparaging. After: remove; if a real difference is supported, state it factually
with its source. (R18, R19, R21)

**C6 The unqualified "safe".** "Safe", "well tolerated" or "no side effects" as a
comparative or standalone claim.
Why: "safe" needs qualification in the UK, EFPIA and Swiss rules; "no side effects" is
excluded outright in Switzerland. After: state the safety finding as reported, with the
label's warnings nearby; balance is `balanced-evidence`. (R19, R21, R24)

**C7 The comparison with standard care that is not standard.** "Better than standard
care" where the comparator was one specific regimen, or placebo.
Why: the comparator defines the claim. After: name the comparator as in the trial.
(R17, R18)

**C8 The public comparison.** Patient or public material suggests the product works as
well as or better than another treatment.
Why: excluded outright in Swiss public advertising; prescription medicines may not be
advertised to the public in the EU at all. After: remove; route to promotional review.
(R11, R15, R24)
