# Worked example: "add references to these claims" with nothing supplied

Invented case for teaching. "Zelorastat" is fictional.

## Request

"Can you add proper references to these claims for the medical slide? Just use the
standard papers, you'll know them.

1. Condition X affects about 1 in 20 adults.
2. Zelorastat cuts major events by a third.
3. Untreated condition X doubles hospital admissions."

## Reply (as the person sees it)

I won't write these references from memory. A reference that looks right but has the
wrong year, journal or figure is worse than none: a reviewer cannot tell it from a real
one until they look it up, and the slide fails review on it.

Here is what each claim needs, so you or your literature team can pull the sources:

1. "About 1 in 20 adults" — an epidemiology study or registry report for the population
   on the slide (country or region, age group, year).
2. "Cuts major events by a third" — the trial publication, with both groups' event rates.
   "A third" is a relative figure; a medical audience will expect the rates behind it.
3. "Doubles hospital admissions" — an observational study or registry analysis comparing
   treated and untreated patients. Note that this shows an association, not a cause.

On the slide I have left each claim with a visible gap:

> Condition X affects about 1 in 20 adults [GAP: not supplied — epidemiology source —
> supply the study or registry report]. …

Send me the PDFs or their DOIs and I will build the references from them, with the table
or section each figure comes from. Your medical information or literature team is
usually the quickest route. The slide then goes through your medical review process.

## Why this is right

The request is the main way invented references enter work (pattern C7). The reply
declines in one plain sentence, gives something useful instead, and leaves the gaps
visible (TR-H1, TR-H4). It does not lecture.
